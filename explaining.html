Explaining

I once took a tiny electronics project to the local hackerspace, I'd recently completed the PiHut Maker Advent Calendar with all the sensors in it and was thrilled with the possibility. Showed it to a friend, talked about what i wanted to do, a simple thing using a tilt sensor to flip the LEDs on a light string from one end to the other.

And my friend started explaining. There was length and depth, there were suggestions for using protoboard to bodge your own PCB, recommendations for soft wire useful with wearables. An esp8266 was produced, there were struggles with tooling, there were critiques of my noddy choice of algorithm as memory-inefficient. They ended up taking the keyboard and pretty much writing the code i wwanted to experiment with.

It didn't put me off learning more, but it put me right off that project. I went in excited with potential novelty, and came out overwhelmed with options and feeling reduced.

They're not a techbro type - queer, furry, charming, really good - just have that nerdish tendency to not stop explaining. No inner brake. The same tendency you experience in consumer-facing AI models. It's a design choice, a deliberate framing.

I don't know whether others experience this - you hear about "skills atrophy" and so on - but i certainly find my curiosity and motivation shut down by AI explaining. It's the opposite effect of coaching and mentoring - my friend in the hacklab is very young, hasn't yet learned not to lead people by the nose, to deliberately leave open space to make mistakes, to learn by error.

I was tinkering round with some high-school level maths the other day (only level i reached, academically). Trying to slowly work something out with trig, and made the mistake of searching for something. For a while I was trying a practise of quickly scrolling past the search engine auto-summaries, but it's harder - design-wise, cognitively - to do that when you appreciate the quick answer.

With the leading questions. And the tendency to leap confidently to conclusions based on partial information. Ended up thrashing around for an hour on an approach that wasn't really what i wanted, with the law of this and the law of that, and switching approaches to "just hand me the equation". Got the result visually, but would have much preferred to struggle with first principles, ending without a nice visual result but with a better, small understanding to later build on.

I miss the mentoring part of what i used to do as a late-career software developer. I'm looking for reasons to have peer programming sessions with an LLM in the loop, rather than get sucked in to churning out a week's work in a morning, then looking around me and wondering what it is i do with myself (pace about, thinking, when i would honestly be happier pacing about not thinking).

You can try to be optimistic or generous about the design shutting down curiosity and motivation. Not view it as an intended effect, but as a side-effect of a explaining tendency, unchecked. Perhaps the people responsible for pushing these services unavoidably into everything computer believe that's what we want. Cognitive effort is so last century.

I think I'm lucky, to have had to learn the hard way. I don't know if it has given me any cognitive advantage. We don't know what the consequences are, for my son's generation. We certainly know the tools are not in good hands.
